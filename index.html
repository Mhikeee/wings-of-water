A celestial maiden is caught on Earth, nearly sacrificed, and dances her way home. Each country tells it differently.

Seven sisters fly down

On Mount Kailasa live seven celestial sisters. They are kinnari: half woman, half bird.

They fly down to bathe in a forest pond. The youngest is the most beautiful. Her name changes with the teller: Monorea, Manora, Manorah.

Cambodia: their father lets them go if they promise to come home when the gongs ring. This telling starts from the sisters' side.

Something is taken

A hunter watches from the trees. When it is time to leave, six sisters rise into the sky. The youngest cannot.

What is taken from her is the first clue to which telling you are hearing.

Cambodia
A scarf
Her magic scarf vanishes. She cries until she faints, and Prince Sothun offers to help.
Laos
A cord
Two naga reward the hunter with a cord for killing a giant. He binds Manora with it.
Thailand
Her wings
The hunter Boontrik hides the youngest kinnari's wings beside the great lake.

A gift for a prince

The hunter sees she is too high-born for him, so he takes her to the palace.

In Thailand he is paid with rank and jewels. In Laos she is traded for treasure. In Cambodia, Prince Sothun was watching at the pond himself. Each time, the prince falls in love and they marry.

"The more the hunter looked at Manorah, the more he realized he could not possess such a great creature"The Tale of Phra Suthon, Thailand

A lie about a dream

War calls the prince away. While he is gone, a man who reads the future says she must die.

He calls her a bad omen. He is lying. Turn the card to see who he is in each telling.

Cambodia: the king and queen choose exile instead of death.

Thailand: the king and queen disagree, but the astrologer is too powerful to ignore.

Her dance becomes her escape

Her last request: her wings, so she can dance the ritual properly before she dies.

The dance is so beautiful that everyone stops to watch. Then she rises into the sky and is gone.

Laos: she flies away crying, hurt that anyone wanted her dead.

Thailand: on her way home she leaves her ring with the hermit Kassapa.

The prince follows

He comes home from war to find her gone, and sets out after her.

7 years, 7 months, 7 days

In the Thai telling, his journey lasts only seven days in heaven: exactly the time she waits for her purifying bath. The hermit Kassapa gives the prince her ring and warns him of five dangers along the road to Mount Krailas.

Poisonous forest
A forest whose air and water are poison to humans.
Giant bird
A giant bird large enough to carry off a man.
Fighting elephant
A fighting elephant blocking the narrow path.
Twin mountain
Twin mountains that clash together, crushing whatever passes between them.
Great giant
A great giant guarding the last stretch before Mount Krailas.

A ring in the water

He drops her ring into a maid's water jar, or into the seventh golden pot of sacred water. It falls onto her, and she knows he has come.

In Thailand there is one last test: seven sisters dressed exactly alike. Can you find Manorah?

Each telling gives different weight to family, royal power, the maiden's own choices, ritual and the supernatural.

Shared threads

Six things appear in all three tellings. Each one is still alive today.

One salt water, one people

"Wansolwara" is a Pidgin term meaning "one salt water", or more broadly "one ocean, one people". In these stories, nature is part of who we are.

These stories remind us that nature is not separate from our identity but something we inherit, celebrate and have a duty to preserve

Bon Om Touk

Cambodia

The Water Festival marks the Tonle Sap River flowing back to the Mekong, leaving rich sediment for crops. Towns race long pirogue boats, a tradition traced to Jayavarman VII in the 12th century.

Boun Pi Mai

Laos

Lao New Year is celebrated with water, blessings and music. People splash water to wash away bad luck, pour water over Buddha images and build sand stupas.

The Anoma River

The Lao telling

Sithon Manora's sisters bathe in the Anoma. In Buddhism it is where Prince Siddhartha renounced the world. Alexander Cunningham identified it with the Aumi River in Uttar Pradesh.

    Rituals that protect the land

    Beyond the festivals, Southeast Asian beliefs still guard forests and rivers. Two of them come from the same countries as our tellings.

    Thailand

    Bueat Pa, tree ordination

    Monks ordain, or dress, a tree. Given the standing of a monk, it can no longer be harmed or cut down for logging.

    The ceremony honours the spirits of the forest, blesses rivers and asks forgiveness for harming the natural world. In the 1980s it became a grassroots resistance against commercial logging in northern Thailand, and communities and activists across the country took it up.

    Laos, and the Lao telling

    The naga

    Half human and half serpent, nagas belong to the water. Snakes appear with the monsoon, so a naga became a sign of rain and good harvests. In Sithon Manora, two naga guard the pool where the story begins.

    • Guardians of the natural world and of life underwater
    • Wise and powerful, living in harmony with nature
    • Symbols of creation and dissolution, fertility, prosperity and protection

    Nature has always been part of our culture.
    How are we protecting it now?

    Is your data killing our sacred waters?

    Pax Silica is a US-led push for semiconductor and technology hubs, with Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam and the Philippines at its center. It is sold as growth, but it makes heavy demands on land, water, energy and people.

    130Mliters of water a dayabout what 600,000 households use
    3 GWof electricitywhich could lock the region into fossil fuels
    20,000 haof land for solarfor its planned 2 GW of solar, competing with farms

    What it puts in the water

    Chip factories release wastewater with fluorides, ammonia, heavy metals and toxic organic compounds. Without advanced treatment, these can seep into groundwater and soil and cause long-term health risks.

    Sources: Ignacio and Pacheco (2026); Mendoza, Science PH (2026); Sandhu et al. (2025); Sebola-Samanyanga (2026)

    What it takes from communities

    As in the celestial maiden tales, water carries the story. Turning farmland and Indigenous Peoples' lands into industrial sites threatens livelihoods and rituals.

    For many communities, identity and land are one: the land is where they keep their histories and their ties to each other. When they are displaced, what is lost cannot be measured by the price of the land.
